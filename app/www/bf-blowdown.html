Tapping through the blowdown — notes

  • Reduced blast means a low melting rate, so the gaps between taps are long — tap when liquid has collected, not on the normal clock.
  • T0 − 12 h to T0: cast on the normal schedule and keep the hearth drained; the hearth must go into the blowdown dry.
  • T0 − 4 h: stop injection and oxygen; take a full cast to dry the hearth.
  • During the blowdown: a 90-min cast may run dry early when little has collected — that is fine; close and wait for the next gap.
  • Final cast (at target stockline): drill and HOLD the taphole open until it blows gas — do not plug it. Stop spray, then blast, then purge.
  • Deep blowdown (reline): after blast off, tap the salamander through a hole cut 0.3–0.6 m below the normal taphole.

Blow-in — after the repair outage

Blow-in is the mirror of blow-down: starting the furnace from the empty, cold condition back to full production. Each step of this guide uses the same inputs from the Inputs tab.

Day −3 to Day −1 · readiness

  • Refractory dried per the drying curve; brick temperature on target at every zone.
  • Blast system, cooling water, N₂, instrumentation and gas analysers proved; bleeders stroked, GCP ready.
  • Start-up burden staged: dry wood / coke stock, blow-in coke (loose, +20–30% of normal rate on dumps 1–3), light ore layers in the top charges only.
  • Cast house dry run — ladles, runners, slag bay, drill & mud gun all tested.

Why the key settings are what they are

Blast while the stockline is in shaft / belly / bosh / below bosh (0.75 / 0.60 / 0.50 / 0.35)

These are the blowdown blast volumes, each as a fraction of normal blast, stepped down as the stockline falls deeper. Blast stays ON so the coke keeps burning and the burden keeps descending, but full blast in a shortening column causes three problems that get worse the lower the stockline goes:

1. Top gas temperature. With a short column there is less burden above the tuyeres to absorb the heat of the rising gas, so the top runs hotter. Less blast = less gas = less heat carried up, so the top is easier to hold under its limit with nitrogen and spray. This is the main reason.

2. Channelling and slips. A short, uneven column is unstable; high blast pushes gas up preferred paths and the burden can hang then slip. Lower blast keeps the descent steadier.

3. Blowing material out of the top. High velocity in a near-empty furnace throws coke and dust into the gas system — just when water is about to go into the gas.

Coke-up of the blowdown burden (+15% deep / +10% partial)

Coke-up means adding extra coke to the burden in the hours before charging stops, so the furnace empties with a coke-rich column. It is expressed as a fraction of the normal coke rate: a furnace at 450 kg/tHM is charged near 517 kg/tHM (deep) through the window.

As the stockline falls the burden carries less heat down, while the hearth must still hold liquid iron and slag as its heat source burns away above it. Extra coke carries heat to the hearth and keeps the shortening column open so gas escapes and liquids drain. The worst outcome of a blowdown is a cold, frozen hearth at the final cast (stuck salamander, ruined blow-in); the coke-up is the main defence.

+15% roughly replaces the injection you stop at T0 − 4 h. Going higher is NOT safer — more coke to burn away means a longer blowdown, more hours at the top-temperature limit and more spray water, for no extra hearth heat. "+25% to be safe" is a classic mistake.

Partial needs less (+10%) because the stockline only reaches the belly — the bosh and hearth stay full of hot burden, so the hearth is never at risk.

How it is charged: the coke rate per hour does not change. The coke-up is made by taking ore out of each charge, not by adding coke dumps — the coke dump stays the same, the ore dump shrinks, ore/coke drops, and only production falls. Start about 12 h before T0 so the coked-up material fills the whole furnace by the time charging stops.

Spray water & nitrogen priority

Nitrogen first, spray second. At the top, nitrogen cools the gas by dilution without adding any H₂ risk. Spray adds free water; some evaporates in the gas (good, cools it) but some reaches the burden surface and reacts with hot coke → CO + H₂. Hold the top with N₂ as long as you can, then use spray in small increments.

Stop rule: if H₂ in top gas exceeds 12%, stop the spray and divert gas to the bleeder. If spray reaches capacity and the top is still above limit, cut blast — do not chase water.

Dry GCP bag limit (260 °C, stepped down)

The bag-filter cloth burns if its temperature exceeds the rated limit. As the stockline falls below the belly the gas gets hotter and wetter; stay ahead of it by stepping the bag limit down (260 → 220 → 180 °C). When you hit 180 and the top is still rising, switch gas off GCP to the bleeder. For wet GCP there is no bag; hold the top gas under the top equipment limit instead.

Top nitrogen flow available

The total at the top is BLT + lances + the three shell platforms (above-tuyere, mid-shaft, throat). Flow at the top is what holds the temperature; flow deeper in the shell cools the burden surface more than the gas and helps less. If your oxygen plant does not have enough N₂ for the whole duration, slow down the plan (lower blast earlier) rather than running out.

Final cast & salamander

At the target stockline the furnace is empty of burden above the bosh. Drill a full-depth cast and hold the taphole open — when it blows gas, the hearth is dry. Do not plug it. Stop spray, then blast, then back-draught and purge. For a reline, tap the salamander through a hole cut 0.3–0.6 m below the normal taphole once the stoves and blast are off.
